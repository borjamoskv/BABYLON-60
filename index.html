The gap

Most agents can act. Few can prove what they knew.

You don't have memory. You have fragments, logs, and optimism.

Agents keep getting better at calling tools and producing plausible output. What they still lack is a durable record of what they knew, when they knew it, and how a decision was reached — one that survives an adversary who can reach the database.

Application logs answer "what happened" for operations. They were never built to answer "prove it" for an auditor. They are mutable, scattered across systems, hard to tie to a single decision, and dependent on the very production access you're trying to hold accountable.

What it is

A memory substrate that keeps evidence, not just state.

BABYLON-60 is a local-first memory and verification layer for AI systems that need to remember, retrieve, and prove what happened. Structured facts and decisions are written as typed records into a hash-chained ledger — HMAC-SHA256 over SQLite in WAL mode — so any silent mutation of history breaks the chain and is caught on verification.

It sits beside your existing stack as an append-only evidence layer, not on top of it as another place for state to drift. Start with a file on disk; reach for anchoring and cloud backends only when the records start to matter to someone who doesn't trust you.

◆ Hash-chained HMAC-SHA256 ◆ SQLite + WAL, local-first ◆ Typed: fact / decision / tool-call / error ◆ Two-phase write-ahead ◆ Deterministic audit export ◆ Python-first · CLI + API
The part every "immutable ledger" skips

A ledger you control is not proof against you.

A private HMAC ledger proves exactly two things: internal integrity and ordering. It does not prove anteriority to a third party — because you hold the key and you hold the storage. Under Article 12 the operator is the regulated party. To a regulator, "trust my own append-only log" is not evidence: in principle the operator could rebuild the entire chain from scratch.

BABYLON-60 closes the gap by anchoring the chain to a clock the operator cannot move: a public commitment through OpenTimestamps, or an RFC 3161 timestamp from an independent authority. After an anchor, everything before it is fixed against an external record — rewriting it means contradicting a party you don't control.

THE LATENCY AXIOM

ΔT_ancla — the interval between anchors — is the maximum window in which history can still be silently rewritten. Anchor frequency isn't a deployment detail; it's the security parameter. Choose ΔT_ancla, and you've chosen how much of the past is still soft.

EU AI Act · Article 12

What the regulation asks for, in the shape it asks for it.

Article 12 requires high-risk AI systems to technically allow the automatic recording of events over the lifetime of the system, for three stated purposes. BABYLON-60 supplies the record layer for each.

Art. 12(2)(a)
Identify situations that may present a risk (Art. 79) or a substantial modification.
Typed risk and error records that carry the input context and the decision path that produced them — reconstructable after the fact, not summarized away.
Art. 12(2)(b)
Facilitate post-market monitoring (Art. 72).
A continuous, exportable trail with stable correlation IDs linking each record to the user-visible action it belongs to.
Art. 12(2)(c)
Support operational monitoring by deployers (Art. 26(5)).
A verifiable, tamper-evident history a deployer can inspect later — without needing to trust privileged access to the production environment.

Article 12 doesn't say "tamper-proof." Logs that can be silently altered say nothing.

The regulation asks for traceability; it leaves integrity to you. But a log you cannot demonstrate was not altered carries close to zero evidentiary weight — and that weight is exactly what a competent authority is testing. BABYLON-60 supplies the evidence layer. Retention policy, access control, and governance remain yours.

Applicable to high-risk (Annex III) systems 2 Aug 2026. · Penalties up to €15M or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. · Buying a third-party system does not move the obligation off the deployer — you remain accountable for log retention and accessibility.
A Digital Omnibus delay (possibly to Dec 2027) has been proposed but not adopted. Until it is, 2 Aug 2026 remains the enforceable date — and the practical expectation is a retained audit trail already running before enforcement, not started on the day.
Failure semantics

No degradar jamás. Antes morir.

On any integrity failure, BABYLON-60 halts. Ledger integrity outranks availability. A system that keeps serving after its evidence is compromised is worse than one that stops — because it manufactures false confidence in records that can no longer be trusted.

Necrosis — the silent failure

A table quietly dropped on schema drift. Evidence gone, and the system carries on as if nothing happened. The worst outcome for a compliance product: an anti-forensic failure that looks like health. This is the failure mode BABYLON-60 is built to make impossible.

Apoptosis — the disciplined halt

Detect the break, refuse to continue, and leave a compensation record as the signal. The system stops loudly and legibly, with the reason attached. It will refuse to boot on a broken chain rather than paper over it.

◆ TamperError → hard refusal to continue ◆ integrity > availability ◆ every halt leaves a reason

Honest scope

What it isn't.

Not a semantic search database

Keep Qdrant or Pinecone for ephemeral RAG chunks. BABYLON-60 stores the decisions and facts worth defending — the ones you'd have to justify later.

Not an observability platform

Keep Datadog or ELK for metrics, traces, and string logs. Different question, different tool. This is the evidence layer, not the dashboard.

Not a hallucination filter

A cryptographically logged lie is still a lie. BABYLON-60 proves what was recorded and that it wasn't altered — not that the model was right.
